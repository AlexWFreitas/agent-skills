# Photosynthesis makes more sense when you separate energy from ingredients

The usual one-line explanation—plants use sunlight to make food—isn't wrong,
but it hides the part that makes photosynthesis click.

Sunlight is the energy source. Carbon dioxide and water are the material
ingredients. A plant uses light energy to rearrange those ingredients into
energy-rich carbon compounds, and oxygen is released along the way.

The familiar summary looks like this:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

Keep it as a summary, not a literal recipe with one step. The real process has
two connected stages, and the equation doesn't show where every atom came from.
The most important hidden detail is that the released oxygen comes from water,
not carbon dioxide.

## First, picture the chloroplast

Photosynthesis happens in **chloroplasts** inside green plant cells. A
chloroplast has folded internal membranes called **thylakoids** surrounded by a
fluid called the **stroma**.

That division gives us a useful map:

- The thylakoid membranes handle the light-dependent reactions.
- The stroma handles the Calvin-Benson cycle.

Chlorophyll sits with the light-capturing machinery in the thylakoid membranes.
It absorbs red and blue light especially well and green light less strongly,
which is one reason leaves look green.

## The light reactions charge the system

When chlorophyll absorbs light, electrons gain energy. Those energized
electrons move through a series of components in the thylakoid membrane.

Here's the part people often miss: water replaces electrons that leave the
light-capturing system. Splitting water produces electrons and hydrogen ions
and releases oxygen. So the oxygen that diffuses out of a leaf started in water
molecules.

As electrons move, the chloroplast builds up hydrogen ions on one side of the
thylakoid membrane. When those ions flow back through ATP synthase, the
chloroplast makes ATP. The reactions also make NADPH, which carries high-energy
electrons.

You don't need to treat ATP and NADPH as mysterious names. For this lesson,
their jobs are enough:

- ATP carries usable chemical energy.
- NADPH carries high-energy electrons.

They are short-term carriers that connect the light reactions to the next
stage. They aren't the final sugar stored by the plant.

## The Calvin cycle builds carbon compounds

The Calvin cycle runs in the stroma. It takes carbon dioxide and uses ATP and
NADPH to incorporate that carbon into organic molecules. This is called
**carbon fixation**.

The direct carbohydrate product is **G3P**, short for glyceraldehyde
3-phosphate. The plant can use carbon compounds derived from G3P to build
glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, and much more. Part of the cycle also
rebuilds the molecule that accepts incoming carbon dioxide, so the cycle can
continue.

This is another place where simplified explanations can mislead. The Calvin
cycle doesn't pop out a finished glucose molecule in one direct step. G3P comes
first, and later pathways assemble larger products.

You may also hear the Calvin cycle called the light-independent reactions. That
doesn't mean it happily runs through the night on its own. It doesn't use
photons directly, but it normally relies on ATP, NADPH, and regulation linked
to the light-dependent reactions.

## Now trace what actually moved

If you keep energy and matter in separate paths, the whole subject becomes
easier to remember.

**Energy**

```text
sunlight -> energized electrons -> ATP and NADPH -> chemical bonds
```

**Water**

```text
water -> electrons and hydrogen ions used by the light reactions + oxygen released
```

**Carbon**

```text
carbon dioxide -> Calvin cycle -> G3P -> sugars and other plant material
```

Light doesn't turn into leaf tissue. It supplies energy. Much of a plant's dry
mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air. The plant still needs water and
minerals from soil, but soil isn't the main source of its carbon mass.

## What happens when conditions change?

Photosynthesis is a chain of dependent reactions, so its rate can be limited in
several places.

- Low light means less energy reaches the photosystems.
- Low carbon dioxide means less carbon is available to fix.
- Temperature affects the enzymes that run the reactions.
- Water shortage affects the process and can cause stomata to close. That saves
  water but also restricts carbon dioxide entry.

More of one factor helps only until another factor becomes the bottleneck—the
limiting factor. A plant in intense light won't keep increasing photosynthesis
if it can't obtain enough carbon dioxide or water.

## Plants still need respiration

Plants don't choose between being photosynthetic organisms and respiring
organisms. They do both.

Photosynthesis stores energy in organic molecules. Cellular respiration
releases usable energy from those molecules for growth, repair, transport, and
other cellular work. Photosynthesis depends on available light; respiration can
continue during the day and at night.

## The version worth remembering

If you want one compact model, use this:

1. In the thylakoid membranes, light-dependent reactions capture light, split
   water, release oxygen, and make ATP and NADPH.
2. In the stroma, the Calvin cycle spends ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide
   into G3P.
3. The plant uses carbon compounds derived from G3P to build sugars and much of
   its body.

That process matters far beyond one leaf. Photosynthesis produces the organic
matter that supports most food webs, contributes much of the atmosphere's
oxygen, and moves carbon dioxide from the air into living material.
