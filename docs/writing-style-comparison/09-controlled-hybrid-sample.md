# Photosynthesis: how light becomes stored chemical energy

Photosynthesis becomes much easier to understand once you separate **energy**
from **matter**. A plant does not turn sunlight into leaves. Light supplies the
energy; carbon dioxide and water supply the atoms that the plant rearranges
into organic molecules.

The familiar summary equation is:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

That equation is useful, but it compresses many reactions into one line. It
also hides an important detail: the oxygen released by photosynthesis comes
from water, not carbon dioxide.

## Two linked stages

Photosynthesis occurs in **chloroplasts** inside green plant cells. A
chloroplast divides the work between two locations:

- The **thylakoid membranes** perform the light-dependent reactions.
- The surrounding **stroma** contains the Calvin-Benson cycle.

Think of these as two linked jobs. The first captures and packages energy. The
second spends that energy to build carbon compounds.

## 1. Capture and package light energy

Chlorophyll and associated photosystems sit in the thylakoid membranes.
Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light especially well and green light less
strongly, which helps explain why leaves appear green.

When chlorophyll absorbs light, electrons gain energy. Water supplies
replacement electrons. Splitting water also contributes hydrogen ions and
releases oxygen.

The energized electrons move through membrane components and help build a
proton concentration difference across the thylakoid membrane. When protons
flow back through ATP synthase, the chloroplast produces ATP. The reactions
also form NADPH, which carries high-energy electrons.

For this lesson, their roles are enough:

- **ATP** carries usable chemical energy.
- **NADPH** carries high-energy electrons.

They are temporary carriers, not the plant's final food. Their job is to move
captured energy and electrons into the next stage.

## 2. Use that energy to fix carbon

The Calvin cycle operates in the stroma. It uses carbon dioxide, ATP, and NADPH
to perform **carbon fixation**: incorporating carbon from an inorganic gas into
organic molecules.

Its direct carbohydrate product is glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, usually called
**G3P**. The plant can use carbon compounds derived from G3P to build glucose,
sucrose, starch, cellulose, and many other substances. Part of the cycle also
regenerates the molecule that accepts incoming carbon dioxide, allowing the
process to continue.

This corrects two common shortcuts. The Calvin cycle does not directly produce
a finished glucose molecule in one step. It also is not truly independent of
light: although it does not absorb photons directly, it normally relies on ATP,
NADPH, and regulation connected to the light-dependent reactions.

## Keep the paths separate

Here is the causal model worth remembering:

```text
Energy: light -> energized electrons -> ATP and NADPH -> chemical bonds
Water:  water -> electrons and hydrogen ions + oxygen released
Carbon: carbon dioxide -> Calvin cycle -> G3P -> plant organic matter
```

Light changes the energy available to the system; it does not provide plant
mass. Much of a plant's dry mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air. Water and
mineral nutrients from soil remain essential, but soil is not the main source
of the plant's carbon.

This model also lets you predict what happens when conditions change:

- Less usable light means less energy reaches the photosystems.
- Less carbon dioxide means the Calvin cycle has less carbon to fix.
- Unsuitable temperature slows or disrupts enzyme-controlled reactions.
- Water stress can close stomata, conserving water but reducing carbon dioxide
  entry.

More of one input helps only until something else becomes the limiting factor.
Extra light cannot compensate indefinitely for too little carbon dioxide or
water.

## Plants also perform cellular respiration

Photosynthesis stores energy in organic molecules. Plants still need to release
usable energy from those molecules for growth, repair, transport, and other
cellular work, so they also perform cellular respiration.

Photosynthesis depends on available light. Respiration can continue during the
day and at night. Plants are not organisms that photosynthesize *instead of*
respiring; they do both for different purposes.

## The compact version

If you want the entire lesson in three points:

1. Light-dependent reactions in thylakoid membranes capture light, split water,
   release oxygen, and make ATP and NADPH.
2. The Calvin cycle in the stroma uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into
   G3P.
3. Carbon compounds derived from G3P become sugars and other plant material,
   supporting most food webs while photosynthesis also contributes atmospheric
   oxygen.

That is the useful center of the process: light energy is converted into
transferable chemical energy, and that energy is used to turn atmospheric
carbon into living material.
