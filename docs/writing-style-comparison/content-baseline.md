# Controlled photosynthesis lesson baseline

This document defines the content that every writing-style sample must preserve.
It is a comparison control, not a ninth style candidate.

## Fixed request and audience

The request is:

> Write a sample lesson that explains photosynthesis to a curious adult who
> remembers only basic school science.

The lesson must be understandable without prior knowledge of biochemistry. It
may introduce technical terms, but it must define them in context.

## Fixed learning objectives

After reading any candidate, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain what photosynthesis accomplishes.
2. Name the chloroplast structures where its two major stages occur.
3. Describe the inputs and outputs of the light-dependent reactions.
4. Describe how the Calvin cycle uses carbon dioxide, ATP, and NADPH.
5. Distinguish the path of energy from the path of matter.
6. Explain why photosynthesis matters to plants and most ecosystems.
7. Correct several common misconceptions about plant growth and metabolism.

## Fixed scope

Every candidate explains oxygen-producing photosynthesis in green land plants.
The lesson focuses on a leaf cell and its chloroplasts. It does not teach the
detailed electron transport chain, enzyme kinetics, C3/C4/CAM pathways,
photorespiration, or the diversity of bacterial photosynthesis.

## Required scientific model

### Overall purpose

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic
molecules. It uses carbon dioxide and water as material inputs and releases
oxygen.

The familiar summary equation is:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

This equation is a useful net shorthand. It does not show the intermediate
reactions or trace the origin of every atom. In oxygen-producing photosynthesis,
the released oxygen comes from water, not from carbon dioxide.

### Relevant structures

- **Chloroplast:** the organelle where photosynthesis occurs in plant cells.
- **Thylakoid membrane:** the internal chloroplast membrane where the
  light-dependent reactions occur.
- **Stroma:** the fluid region around the thylakoids where the Calvin cycle
  occurs.
- **Chlorophyll:** a pigment that absorbs light, especially in red and blue
  regions of the visible spectrum. Leaves appear green because chlorophyll
  absorbs green light less strongly than those wavelengths.

### Light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes:

1. Chlorophyll and associated photosystems absorb light energy.
2. Water supplies electrons and hydrogen ions. Splitting water releases oxygen.
3. Electron movement helps create a proton gradient across the membrane.
4. The gradient drives ATP production, and energized electrons help form NADPH.

ATP and NADPH are temporary chemical carriers. ATP supplies usable energy, and
NADPH supplies high-energy electrons. They connect the light-dependent
reactions to carbon fixation.

### Calvin cycle

The Calvin-Benson cycle occurs in the stroma. It does not use photons directly,
but it depends on ATP and NADPH made by the light-dependent reactions.

The cycle fixes carbon dioxide by incorporating its carbon into organic
molecules. Its direct carbohydrate product is glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P),
which the plant can use to build glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, and many
other compounds. The cycle also regenerates the carbon-accepting molecule that
allows carbon fixation to continue.

### Energy and matter

The lesson must keep these paths distinct:

- **Energy path:** sunlight -> energized electrons -> ATP and NADPH -> chemical
  bonds in organic molecules.
- **Water path:** water -> electrons and hydrogen ions used in the light
  reactions, plus oxygen released as a by-product.
- **Carbon path:** carbon dioxide -> carbon fixed by the Calvin cycle -> G3P ->
  sugars and other plant material.

Light provides energy. Light does not become plant matter. Much of a plant's dry
mass comes from carbon dioxide taken from the air, while water and mineral
nutrients from the soil remain essential.

### Relationship to cellular respiration

Plants perform cellular respiration as well as photosynthesis. Photosynthesis
stores energy in organic molecules. Cellular respiration releases usable energy
from those molecules for cellular work. A plant can respire day and night;
photosynthesis depends on available light.

### Environmental influences

The photosynthetic rate can be constrained by:

- Light intensity and usable wavelengths.
- Carbon dioxide availability.
- Temperature, because photosynthesis depends on enzyme-controlled reactions.
- Water availability. Water is a reactant, and water stress can close stomata,
  reducing carbon dioxide entry into a leaf.

Increasing one factor stops helping when another factor becomes limiting.

### Biological importance

Photosynthesis creates organic matter that supports most food webs, directly or
indirectly. It is also a major source of atmospheric oxygen and removes carbon
dioxide from the atmosphere while carbon is being fixed.

## Misconceptions every candidate must correct

- Plants do not obtain most of their dry mass from soil.
- Released oxygen comes from water, not carbon dioxide.
- The Calvin cycle does not require photons directly, but it normally depends
  on products and regulation associated with the light-dependent reactions.
- The Calvin cycle produces G3P directly; glucose is a later product assembled
  from carbon compounds.
- Plants do not only photosynthesize. They also perform cellular respiration.
- Photosynthesis does not convert matter into energy. It uses light energy to
  rearrange matter into higher-energy chemical structures.

## What may vary by style

Candidates may vary:

- Opening strategy and tone.
- Order of presentation.
- Paragraph and sentence length.
- Use of headings, lists, questions, narrative, or analogy.
- Amount of repetition and use of learning checks.
- Closing strategy.

An analogy or story must be mapped back to the scientific terms and must state
where the comparison stops matching reality.
