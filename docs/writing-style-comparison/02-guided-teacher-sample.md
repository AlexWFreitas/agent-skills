# Learn how photosynthesis turns light into stored chemical energy

Photosynthesis can seem like one mysterious reaction: a plant receives sunlight
and somehow produces sugar and oxygen. The process becomes much easier to
understand when you separate it into two connected stages.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain where those stages
occur, what each stage contributes, and how energy and matter move through the
process.

## Begin with the big picture

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic
molecules. For green land plants, the familiar net summary is:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

Treat this equation as a summary ledger. It shows the overall inputs and
outputs, but it hides many intermediate reactions. It also does not reveal an
important fact: the oxygen released into the air comes from water, not carbon
dioxide.

### Check your starting model

Ask yourself what the equation says about two different things:

- **Matter:** carbon dioxide and water provide atoms that are rearranged.
- **Energy:** light supplies the energy that makes the rearrangement possible.

Light does not turn into plant matter. It changes the energy state of matter.

## Step 1: Locate the process

Photosynthesis occurs in **chloroplasts**, organelles inside green plant cells.
A chloroplast contains two regions that matter for this lesson:

- **Thylakoid membranes:** folded internal membranes that contain chlorophyll,
  photosystems, electron carriers, and ATP-producing machinery.
- **Stroma:** the fluid surrounding the thylakoids, where the enzymes of the
  Calvin cycle operate.

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light more strongly than green light. Because
green light is absorbed less strongly, leaves appear green.

Keep this location map in mind: thylakoids capture and package light energy;
the stroma uses that packaged energy to build carbon compounds.

## Step 2: Follow the light-dependent reactions

The first stage occurs in the thylakoid membranes and requires light directly.

1. Chlorophyll and associated photosystems absorb photons.
2. That energy raises electrons to higher energy states.
3. Water supplies replacement electrons. Splitting water also produces
   hydrogen ions and releases oxygen.
4. Electron movement helps create a proton concentration difference across the
   thylakoid membrane.
5. As protons flow back across the membrane through ATP synthase, the chloroplast
   produces ATP. The reactions also produce NADPH, a carrier of high-energy
   electrons.

ATP and NADPH are temporary carriers, not the final food made by the plant. ATP
provides usable chemical energy. NADPH provides energized electrons. The next
stage uses both.

### Checkpoint

Where does the released oxygen come from?

It comes from water molecules split during the light-dependent reactions. If
you remember only one correction to the simplified equation, remember this one.

## Step 3: Follow the Calvin cycle

The Calvin-Benson cycle occurs in the stroma. It does not use photons directly,
which is why it has sometimes been called light-independent. That name can be
misleading: the cycle normally depends on ATP and NADPH made by the
light-dependent reactions, and several of its enzymes are regulated by light-
associated conditions.

The cycle performs **carbon fixation**. It incorporates carbon from carbon
dioxide into organic molecules. Using ATP and NADPH, the cycle produces
glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, usually shortened to **G3P**.

G3P is the direct carbohydrate product of the cycle. The plant can use it to
build glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, and many other organic compounds.
Part of the cycle's work also regenerates the molecule that accepts new carbon
dioxide, allowing fixation to continue.

### Checkpoint

Does the Calvin cycle directly produce a finished glucose molecule?

No. It produces G3P. The plant uses carbon compounds such as G3P in later
pathways to assemble glucose and other molecules.

## Step 4: Connect the two stages

The two stages exchange chemical carriers:

1. Light-dependent reactions use light and water to produce ATP and NADPH and
   release oxygen.
2. The Calvin cycle spends ATP and NADPH to incorporate carbon dioxide into
   G3P.
3. The lower-energy carrier forms return to the light-dependent reactions and
   can be charged again.

This gives you two paths to track:

- **Energy:** light -> energized electrons -> ATP and NADPH -> chemical bonds
  in carbon compounds.
- **Matter:** water contributes electrons and hydrogen ions and releases
  oxygen; carbon dioxide supplies the carbon that becomes plant material.

Much of a plant's dry mass therefore comes from carbon dioxide in the air, not
from soil. Soil still supplies essential water and mineral nutrients, but it is
not the main source of the plant's carbon.

## Step 5: Predict what changes the rate

The rate of photosynthesis depends on several interacting conditions:

- More usable light can increase the rate until another resource becomes
  limiting.
- More carbon dioxide can help until light, temperature, or another factor
  becomes limiting.
- Temperature affects the enzyme-controlled reactions, so both low and high
  temperatures can reduce the rate.
- Water shortage can slow photosynthesis directly and can cause stomata to
  close. Closed stomata conserve water but reduce carbon dioxide entry.

The phrase *limiting factor* means the condition that most constrains the
process at that moment. Improving a nonlimiting factor might have little effect.

## Step 6: Place photosynthesis in the plant's life

Plants also perform cellular respiration. Photosynthesis stores energy in
organic molecules; cellular respiration releases usable energy from those
molecules for cellular work. Respiration can continue day and night, while
photosynthesis requires available light.

Photosynthesis supports most food webs by creating organic matter that other
organisms consume directly or indirectly. It is also a major source of
atmospheric oxygen and removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere while carbon
is being fixed.
