# Photosynthesis in green plants

## Definition and significance

Photosynthesis is the set of biochemical processes by which photosynthetic
organisms convert light energy into chemical energy and use that energy to
synthesize organic compounds. In green land plants, carbon dioxide and water
provide the principal material inputs, and molecular oxygen is released.

The common net equation is:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

This equation is a simplified net representation. It omits intermediate
reactions and does not trace atomic origins. Isotopic and biochemical evidence
shows that the oxygen released during oxygenic photosynthesis originates from
water rather than carbon dioxide.

Photosynthesis supplies organic matter to most food webs and is a major source
of atmospheric oxygen. Carbon fixation also removes carbon dioxide from the
atmosphere while the carbon remains in organic matter.

## Chloroplast organization

Photosynthesis in green plant cells occurs in chloroplasts. Chloroplast
structure separates the two major reaction groups.

| Structure | Relevant function |
| --- | --- |
| Thylakoid membrane | Contains chlorophyll, photosystems, electron-transfer components, and ATP synthase; site of the light-dependent reactions |
| Thylakoid lumen | Internal compartment in which hydrogen ions accumulate during light-dependent electron transfer |
| Stroma | Fluid region surrounding the thylakoids; site of the Calvin-Benson cycle |

Chlorophyll pigments absorb light most effectively in red and blue regions of
the visible spectrum and less effectively in the green region. The weaker
absorption of green light contributes to the green appearance of leaves.

## Light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions transform light energy into chemical energy in
the forms of ATP and NADPH. They occur in the thylakoid membranes.

### Light absorption and water oxidation

Chlorophyll and associated pigments form photosystems that absorb photons.
Absorption raises electrons to higher energy states. Water supplies replacement
electrons. The oxidation of water also produces hydrogen ions and releases
molecular oxygen.

### Electron transfer and ATP formation

Energized electrons move through a sequence of electron carriers. Electron
transfer contributes to the accumulation of hydrogen ions in the thylakoid
lumen. The resulting proton gradient stores potential energy.

Hydrogen ions move from the lumen to the stroma through ATP synthase. This flow
drives the phosphorylation of ADP to form ATP.

### NADPH formation

Electrons energized by the photosystems ultimately contribute to the reduction
of NADP+ to NADPH. NADPH carries high-energy electrons. ATP and NADPH are
short-term carriers that provide energy and reducing power for carbon fixation.

## Calvin-Benson cycle

The Calvin-Benson cycle occurs in the chloroplast stroma. It does not require
photons as direct reactants, but it depends on ATP and NADPH produced by the
light-dependent reactions and on regulatory conditions associated with light.
The term *light-independent reactions* therefore does not imply unrestricted
operation in darkness.

The cycle has three broad functions:

1. **Carbon fixation:** carbon dioxide is incorporated into an organic
   intermediate.
2. **Reduction:** ATP and NADPH help convert fixed carbon into glyceraldehyde
   3-phosphate (G3P).
3. **Regeneration:** part of the cycle regenerates the carbon dioxide acceptor,
   allowing fixation to continue.

G3P is the direct carbohydrate product that can leave the cycle. Plants use
carbon compounds derived from G3P to synthesize glucose, sucrose, starch,
cellulose, and many other organic molecules. A complete glucose molecule is not
the direct product of one turn of the Calvin cycle.

## Energy and material accounting

Photosynthesis couples energy conversion to matter transformation.

### Energy pathway

```text
light energy
-> excited electrons
-> proton gradient, ATP, and NADPH
-> chemical energy in organic molecules
```

Energy is transferred and transformed. Light does not become matter.

### Water pathway

Water participates in the light-dependent reactions. It supplies electrons and
hydrogen ions, and its oxidation releases oxygen.

### Carbon pathway

Carbon dioxide supplies carbon to the Calvin cycle. Fixed carbon enters G3P and
subsequently becomes part of carbohydrates and other plant compounds. Much of
a plant's dry mass therefore derives from atmospheric carbon dioxide rather
than soil. Water and mineral nutrients from soil remain essential for plant
structure and metabolism.

## Environmental controls

The rate of photosynthesis depends on interacting environmental variables.

- **Light:** intensity and wavelength affect the energy available to the
  photosystems.
- **Carbon dioxide:** availability affects the substrate supply for carbon
  fixation.
- **Temperature:** enzyme-controlled reactions have temperature-dependent
  rates and functional ranges.
- **Water:** water is a reactant, and water deficit can induce stomatal closure,
  which reduces carbon dioxide entry.

An increase in one variable raises the photosynthetic rate only while that
variable is the limiting factor. When another variable becomes more
restrictive, further increase produces little or no additional effect.

## Relationship to cellular respiration

Plants perform both photosynthesis and cellular respiration. Photosynthesis
stores energy in organic compounds. Cellular respiration releases usable
energy from organic compounds to support cellular work.

Photosynthesis requires available light, whereas respiration can continue in
light or darkness. It is therefore incorrect to describe plants as organisms
that photosynthesize instead of respiring.

## Summary

The light-dependent reactions in thylakoid membranes absorb light, oxidize
water, release oxygen, and generate ATP and NADPH. The Calvin-Benson cycle in the
stroma uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide and produce G3P. These coupled
processes transform light energy into stored chemical energy and inorganic
carbon into organic matter.

## Review questions

1. Which chloroplast structure contains the light-dependent reactions?
2. Why is the phrase *light-independent reactions* potentially misleading?
3. What molecule is the source of released oxygen?
4. What direct carbohydrate product leaves the Calvin cycle?
5. Why does increasing light eventually stop increasing photosynthesis?

Answers: thylakoid membranes; the Calvin cycle depends indirectly on light
through ATP, NADPH, and regulation; water; G3P; another factor becomes limiting.
