# Photosynthesis as two coupled flows

The cleanest way to understand photosynthesis is to separate two things that
are often blended together: **energy flows through the system, while matter is
rearranged by the system**.

Light is the energy source. Water and carbon dioxide are matter. The plant uses
light energy to move electrons into higher-energy arrangements and to build
organic molecules from carbon dioxide.

The familiar equation summarizes the net result:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

The equation balances inputs and outputs, but it hides the mechanism. It also
does not reveal that the released oxygen comes from water. To see why the
process works, follow energy and carbon through two coupled subsystems.

## System 1: Convert light into transferable chemical energy

This subsystem occupies the **thylakoid membranes** inside a chloroplast.
Chlorophyll and associated photosystems absorb light, especially in red and
blue regions of the visible spectrum. Chlorophyll absorbs green light less
strongly, which helps explain why leaves appear green.

Absorbed light raises electrons to higher energy states. Those electrons cannot
simply leave without replacement, so water supplies new electrons. Splitting
water also produces hydrogen ions and releases oxygen.

The energized electrons pass through membrane components. Their movement helps
build a proton concentration difference across the thylakoid membrane. Protons
then flow through ATP synthase, which produces ATP. The light reactions also
load high-energy electrons onto NADPH.

The causal chain is:

```text
light absorption
-> energized electrons
-> electron transport and a proton gradient
-> ATP and NADPH
```

ATP carries usable chemical energy. NADPH carries high-energy electrons. These
carriers are temporary outputs of the light-dependent reactions and inputs to
the second subsystem.

## System 2: Convert inorganic carbon into organic carbon

This subsystem is the **Calvin-Benson cycle**, which operates in the
chloroplast's **stroma**, the fluid around the thylakoids.

The cycle performs carbon fixation: it incorporates carbon from carbon dioxide
into organic molecules. That transformation requires energy and high-energy
electrons, which ATP and NADPH provide.

The direct carbohydrate product is glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, or **G3P**. Some
G3P can leave the cycle and contribute to glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose,
and other compounds. The rest of the cycle's activity regenerates the molecule
that accepts carbon dioxide, allowing fixation to continue.

The causal chain is:

```text
carbon dioxide + ATP + NADPH
-> carbon fixation and reduction
-> G3P
-> sugars and other organic molecules
```

The Calvin cycle does not use photons directly, but it normally depends on ATP,
NADPH, and regulatory conditions produced by the light-dependent reactions.
Calling it *light-independent* therefore does not mean that it is a separate
nighttime process.

## Join the two systems

The two subsystems are coupled by chemical carriers:

```text
thylakoid light reactions --ATP and NADPH--> Calvin cycle in the stroma
thylakoid light reactions <--lower-energy carrier forms-- Calvin cycle
```

This relationship explains several observations.

**If light disappears,** ATP and NADPH production falls. The Calvin cycle may
continue briefly while carriers remain available, but it cannot continue at
the same rate indefinitely.

**If carbon dioxide becomes scarce,** the light reactions might still capture
energy, but carbon fixation becomes constrained. More light alone cannot remove
the carbon shortage.

**If water stress closes stomata,** the leaf conserves water but admits less
carbon dioxide. Carbon fixation then slows even if sunlight is abundant.

## Account for matter

The matter paths are different from the energy path:

- Water is oxidized in the light reactions. It supplies electrons and hydrogen
  ions, and molecular oxygen is released.
- Carbon dioxide supplies the carbon that the Calvin cycle incorporates into
  G3P and later organic molecules.
- Mineral nutrients from soil are essential for plant structure and enzymes,
  but much of the plant's dry mass comes from atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Light is not an ingredient that gains mass inside the plant. It changes which
chemical arrangements are possible.

## Account for stored and released energy

Photosynthesis stores energy in organic molecules. A plant still needs a way to
release usable energy from those molecules, so it also performs cellular
respiration.

Photosynthesis and respiration are not mutually exclusive identities of a
plant. Photosynthesis depends on available light; respiration can continue day
and night. One process builds energy-rich carbon compounds, and the other helps
extract usable energy from them for cellular work.

## Predict the photosynthetic rate

A photosynthetic rate is set by interacting constraints rather than one master
control:

- Light intensity and wavelength determine how much usable light reaches the
  photosystems.
- Carbon dioxide availability constrains carbon fixation.
- Temperature affects enzyme-controlled reactions.
- Water availability affects the light reactions and stomatal gas exchange.

Increasing one input raises the rate only until another condition becomes the
limiting factor. This is why the statement "more light means more photosynthesis" is
true only within a particular range and set of conditions.

## Why the coupled model matters

Photosynthesis supplies organic matter to most food webs and contributes much
of the oxygen in the atmosphere. Its ecological importance follows directly
from the two flows: light energy becomes stored chemical energy, and inorganic
carbon becomes organic material that organisms can use.

If you can trace those two flows without mixing them, the process stops looking
like a single magical equation:

- Light reactions transform energy and release oxygen from water.
- The Calvin cycle uses that transformed energy to fix carbon dioxide into G3P.
- Later pathways turn those carbon compounds into the plant's diverse
  materials.
