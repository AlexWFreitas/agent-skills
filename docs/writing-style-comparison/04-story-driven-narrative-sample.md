# A journey through a sunlit leaf

Morning light reaches a leaf. Some wavelengths are reflected or transmitted,
while some photons meet chlorophyll inside a cell's chloroplasts.
Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light especially well and absorbs green light
less strongly, which is part of why the leaf looks green to us.

One absorbed photon begins a chain of energy transfers. To follow that chain,
we will trace three travelers through the leaf: light arriving from the Sun, a
water molecule drawn up from the roots, and a carbon dioxide molecule entering
from the air.

## The photon reaches the thylakoid

Inside a chloroplast, folded membranes called **thylakoids** hold chlorophyll,
photosystems, electron carriers, and ATP-producing machinery. When chlorophyll
absorbs light, the energy raises an electron to a higher energy state.

The photon does not become part of the plant. Its energy changes the electron's
state and sets a chemical process in motion.

The energized electron moves away through a sequence of carriers. Its movement
helps create a difference in proton concentration across the thylakoid
membrane. Like water stored behind a dam, that concentration difference can do
work when protons flow back across the membrane. They pass through ATP synthase,
which produces ATP.

Another carrier, NADPH, receives high-energy electrons. ATP now carries usable
chemical energy, and NADPH carries reducing power in the form of energized
electrons. The light-dependent reactions have packaged the photon's energy into
forms that another part of the chloroplast can use.

## The water molecule is split

The electron that left chlorophyll must be replaced. A water molecule supplies
replacement electrons during the light-dependent reactions. Splitting water
also contributes hydrogen ions and releases oxygen.

That detail corrects a common misunderstanding. The oxygen leaving a
photosynthesizing leaf comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. Water has
therefore done more than keep the plant hydrated: some of its atoms and
electrons have entered the energy-conversion process, while oxygen has been
released as a by-product.

The released oxygen can diffuse out of the leaf. Across many plants, algae, and
cyanobacteria, oxygen-producing photosynthesis is a major source of the oxygen
in Earth's atmosphere.

## The carbon dioxide molecule enters the stroma

While the light reactions operate in the thylakoid membranes, carbon dioxide
enters the leaf through small pores called stomata. It reaches the chloroplast's
**stroma**, the fluid region surrounding the thylakoids.

Here the carbon dioxide molecule enters the Calvin-Benson cycle. The cycle does
not absorb the photon directly. Instead, it uses ATP and NADPH produced by the
light-dependent reactions.

The carbon atom is **fixed**: it becomes part of an organic molecule. Through a
series of enzyme-controlled reactions, the cycle produces glyceraldehyde
3-phosphate, or **G3P**. Some G3P leaves the cycle and can contribute to glucose,
sucrose, starch, cellulose, and many other plant compounds. The rest helps
regenerate the molecule that accepts incoming carbon dioxide, allowing the
cycle to continue.

The carbon atom that entered as an invisible gas may later become part of a
leaf, a root, a seed, a piece of wood, or a molecule consumed by another
organism. Much of a plant's dry mass begins as carbon dioxide in the air, not as
material absorbed from soil. Water and soil minerals remain essential, but the
air supplies most of the carbon framework.

## The three journeys meet

The textbook equation compresses the story into one line:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

The equation is useful, but the journey shows what it hides:

- Light contributes energy, not matter.
- Water supplies electrons and hydrogen ions, and its splitting releases
  oxygen.
- Carbon dioxide supplies carbon that the Calvin cycle incorporates into G3P.
- Glucose is not produced in one direct Calvin-cycle step; it is assembled
  later from carbon compounds.

The phrase *light-independent reactions* can also mislead. The Calvin cycle
does not use photons directly, but it normally depends on ATP, NADPH, and
regulatory conditions connected to the light-dependent reactions. It is not
simply a second process reserved for nighttime.

## The leaf responds to its surroundings

The journey changes when conditions change.

On a dim day, fewer usable photons reach the photosystems. When carbon dioxide
is scarce, the Calvin cycle has less carbon to fix. Temperature affects the
enzymes that run the reactions. During water stress, stomata may close to reduce
water loss, but that also restricts carbon dioxide entry.

No single input controls the rate in every situation. More light helps only
until carbon dioxide, temperature, water, or another condition becomes the
limiting factor.

## The plant uses what it built

Photosynthesis stores energy in organic molecules, but the plant still needs to
release usable energy for growth, transport, repair, and reproduction. It does
that through cellular respiration, the same broad energy-releasing process
used by animals and many other organisms.

Plants therefore both photosynthesize and respire. Photosynthesis depends on
available light. Respiration can continue during the day and night.

The morning journey through one leaf scales outward. Carbon fixed by
photosynthesis becomes the organic matter that supports most food webs. The
oxygen released from water supports aerobic life. The process links sunlight,
air, water, plants, and the organisms that depend on them.

## Where the story stops

Photons, water molecules, and carbon atoms do not choose destinations. The
narrative is a way to follow energy and matter through a complex network of
reactions. The exact process involves many molecules and regulated steps beyond
this introductory journey.

The scientific core remains simple: light-dependent reactions in thylakoid
membranes make ATP and NADPH and release oxygen from water; the Calvin cycle in
the stroma uses those carriers to fix carbon dioxide into G3P.
