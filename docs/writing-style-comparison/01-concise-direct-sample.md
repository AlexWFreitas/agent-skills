# Photosynthesis, explained directly

Photosynthesis is the process that lets plants use light energy to build
energy-rich organic molecules from carbon dioxide and water. Oxygen is released
in the process.

The familiar summary equation is:

```text
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
```

This is a net shorthand, not a step-by-step reaction. In particular, the oxygen
released by photosynthesis comes from water, not carbon dioxide.

## Where it happens

Photosynthesis occurs in **chloroplasts**, organelles found in green plant
cells. Two chloroplast regions divide the work:

- **Thylakoid membranes** contain chlorophyll and the machinery for the
  light-dependent reactions.
- The **stroma**, the fluid around the thylakoids, contains the enzymes of the
  Calvin cycle.

Chlorophyll absorbs light most strongly in red and blue regions of the visible
spectrum. It absorbs green light less strongly, which contributes to the green
appearance of leaves.

## Stage 1: Capture light energy

The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes.

1. Chlorophyll and associated photosystems absorb light.
2. Water is split to replace electrons energized by that light. This releases
   oxygen and hydrogen ions.
3. Electron movement helps build a proton gradient across the membrane.
4. The gradient drives the production of ATP, while energized electrons help
   form NADPH.

ATP is a short-term carrier of usable chemical energy. NADPH carries
high-energy electrons. Neither is the final food product; both transfer the
captured energy into the second stage.

## Stage 2: Fix carbon

The Calvin-Benson cycle occurs in the stroma. It does not absorb photons
directly, but it normally depends on ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent
reactions.

The cycle incorporates carbon from carbon dioxide into organic molecules. Its
direct carbohydrate product is **G3P**, or glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. A plant
can use G3P to build glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, and many other
compounds. The cycle also regenerates its carbon-accepting molecule so the
process can continue.

## Track energy and matter separately

| What is tracked | Path through photosynthesis |
| --- | --- |
| Energy | Light -> energized electrons -> ATP and NADPH -> chemical bonds in organic molecules |
| Water | Water -> electrons and hydrogen ions used in the light reactions, plus oxygen released |
| Carbon | Carbon dioxide -> Calvin cycle -> G3P -> sugars and other plant material |

Light supplies energy; it does not become plant tissue. Much of a plant's dry
mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air. Water and mineral nutrients from the
soil are still essential, but soil is not the main source of that carbon mass.

## What changes the rate

Photosynthesis can be limited by light intensity and usable wavelengths,
carbon dioxide availability, temperature, or water availability. Water stress
can cause stomata to close, which conserves water but also reduces carbon
dioxide entry into a leaf.

Increasing one factor eventually stops helping if another factor becomes the
limiting factor. More light, for example, cannot compensate indefinitely for
too little carbon dioxide or an unsuitable temperature.

## What photosynthesis is not

- It does not convert matter into energy. It uses light energy to rearrange
  matter into higher-energy chemical structures.
- It does not make glucose in one direct step. The Calvin cycle first produces
  G3P, which contributes to later carbohydrate synthesis.
- It does not replace cellular respiration. Plants respire day and night to
  release usable energy from organic molecules; photosynthesis stores energy
  when light is available.
- The term *light-independent reactions* does not mean that the Calvin cycle
  simply runs all night. It depends on products and regulation associated with
  the light-dependent reactions.

## Why it matters

Photosynthesis creates the organic matter that supports most food webs,
directly or indirectly. It is also a major source of atmospheric oxygen and
removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere while carbon is fixed into organic
matter.

The shortest accurate summary is: the light-dependent reactions charge ATP and
NADPH and release oxygen from water; the Calvin cycle spends ATP and NADPH to
turn carbon dioxide into carbon compounds that the plant can use.
